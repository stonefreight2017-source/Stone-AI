## Seed Classification
- **Domain**: Finance & Fundraising
- **Complexity**: Advanced
- **Applicability**: Pre-seed to Series A SaaS companies
- **Last Updated**: 2026-03-09

## Why Investor Prep Starts Before You Need Money

The worst time to prepare for fundraising is when you need cash in 30 days. The best time is 6-12 months before you plan to raise. Investor prep is about building the narrative, cleaning your data, and creating materials that make investors say "yes" before they finish reading.

Even if you never raise venture capital, the discipline of preparing investor materials makes you a better operator. It forces you to understand your own business at a level that most founders avoid.

## Pitch Deck Structure

### The 12-Slide Framework

This structure works for pre-seed through Series A. It covers everything an investor needs to make an initial assessment. Each slide has ONE job — don't overload them.

### Slide 1: Title

- Company name and logo
- One-line description (what you do, for whom)
- Your name and title
- Contact information
- "Confidential" watermark (signals professionalism)

**Example:**
```
Stone AI
AI-Powered Productivity Agents for Everyone
[Your Name], Founder & CEO
[email] | [phone]
Raising: $X Pre-Seed/Seed
```

### Slide 2: Problem

- Define the problem clearly in 2-3 sentences
- Quantify the pain (time wasted, money lost, opportunity cost)
- Make it personal — tell a story about a real person experiencing this problem
- The problem must be big enough to justify a venture-scale business

**Framework:**
"[Target customer] struggles with [specific problem], costing them [quantified impact]. Current solutions [fail because X]."

### Slide 3: Solution

- What you've built and how it solves the problem
- Show, don't tell — screenshots, demo video thumbnail, product mockup
- Keep it simple: problem → your solution → outcome
- No technical jargon unless pitching to technical investors

### Slide 4: Demo / Product

- Product screenshots or brief walkthrough
- If presenting live, this is where you do a 2-minute demo
- If sending the deck, embed a 60-second Loom video link
- Show the "aha moment" — the point where users see the value

### Slide 5: Market Size

- TAM (Total Addressable Market): The entire market for your category
- SAM (Serviceable Addressable Market): The segment you can realistically serve
- SOM (Serviceable Obtainable Market): What you can capture in 3-5 years

**Bottom-up calculation (preferred):**
```
Example:
- 30M knowledge workers in US use AI tools → TAM: $36B
- 5M actively seeking AI agent platforms → SAM: $6B
- 500K we can reach with our GTM → SOM: $600M
```

Top-down calculations ("The market is $100B, we just need 1%") are a red flag. Always include bottom-up.

### Slide 6: Traction

This is the most important slide. Numbers talk.

**Pre-revenue metrics:**
- Waitlist signups
- Beta users and engagement
- User growth rate (week over week or month over month)
- Key engagement metrics (sessions/user, time in app, feature adoption)

**Post-revenue metrics:**
- MRR and MRR growth rate
- Number of paying customers
- Customer acquisition cost (CAC)
- Lifetime value (LTV)
- LTV:CAC ratio
- Net revenue retention
- Churn rate

**Present traction as a graph.** Up-and-to-the-right is what investors want to see. If your growth is strong, this slide sells the deal.

### Slide 7: Business Model

- How you make money (subscription tiers, pricing)
- Show the pricing page or tier breakdown
- Unit economics: Revenue per customer, COGS per customer, gross margin
- Expansion revenue opportunities (upsells, add-ons)

**Example:**
```
Pricing Tiers:
FREE: $0 (4 agents) → Conversion funnel
STARTER: $19.99/mo (16 agents)
PLUS: $49.99/mo (30 agents)
SMART: $99.99/mo (39 agents) | Annual: $79.99/mo
PRO: $200/mo (42 agents) | Annual: $170/mo

Blended ARPU: $XX/mo
Gross Margin: XX%
LTV: $XXX
CAC: $XX
LTV:CAC: X:1
```

### Slide 8: Go-to-Market Strategy

- How you acquire customers today
- How you'll scale customer acquisition
- Channel breakdown (organic, paid, partnerships, virality)
- Customer journey: awareness → signup → activation → payment

### Slide 9: Competitive Landscape

- Honest assessment of competitors
- 2x2 matrix positioning you favorably (but honestly)
- Your unfair advantage / moat
- Why existing solutions fall short

**2x2 matrix example:**
```
            High Capability
                 │
    [Enterprise] │ [You]
                 │
Low ─────────────┼───────────── High
Accessibility    │              Accessibility
                 │
    [Legacy]     │ [Simple Tools]
                 │
            Low Capability
```

### Slide 10: Team

- Founder(s) with relevant background highlights
- Key team members and their domain expertise
- Advisory board (if notable)
- Key hires you plan to make with funding

**Focus on:**
- Why is THIS team uniquely positioned to solve THIS problem?
- Domain expertise, technical depth, relevant experience
- Previous exits or notable company experience (if applicable)

### Slide 11: Financials & Projections

- Historical revenue (if any)
- 3-year projection with clear assumptions
- Key assumptions stated explicitly
- Path to profitability or next milestone

**Format:**
| Metric | 2025 (Actual) | 2026 (Projected) | 2027 (Projected) | 2028 (Projected) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| MRR (Dec) | $X | $X | $X | $X |
| ARR | $X | $X | $X | $X |
| Customers | X | X | X | X |
| Gross Margin | X% | X% | X% | X% |
| Burn Rate | $X/mo | $X/mo | $X/mo | $X/mo |

### Slide 12: The Ask

- How much you're raising
- What type of round (pre-seed, seed, Series A)
- What the money will be used for (hiring, marketing, infrastructure)
- Key milestones this funding will achieve
- Timeline to next raise (if applicable)

**Use of funds breakdown:**
```
Raising: $500K Pre-Seed

Allocation:
├── Engineering (2 hires): 45%
├── Marketing & Growth: 25%
├── Infrastructure: 15%
└── Operations & Legal: 15%

Milestones:
• 1,000 paying customers
• $50K MRR
• Series Seed ready in 18 months
```

## Financial Projections

### Building Credible Projections

Investors have seen thousands of hockey-stick projections. They're looking for:
1. **Reasonable assumptions** clearly stated
2. **Bottom-up math** that adds up
3. **Understanding of unit economics**
4. **Awareness of costs** (not just revenue)

### Revenue Projection Model

**Bottom-up approach:**

```
Monthly new signups (organic): 200
Monthly new signups (paid): 100
Free-to-paid conversion rate: 5%
New paying customers/month: 15

Average revenue per customer: $65/mo
Monthly churn rate: 5%

Month 1: 15 paying customers × $65 = $975 MRR
Month 2: (15 × 0.95) + 15 new = 29.25 × $65 = $1,901 MRR
Month 3: (29.25 × 0.95) + 15 new = 42.79 × $65 = $2,781 MRR
...
Month 12: ~$8,500 MRR
Month 24: ~$22,000 MRR (with improving conversion and reducing churn)
```

**State your assumptions explicitly:**
- "We assume 5% free-to-paid conversion based on current data / industry benchmarks"
- "We assume 5% monthly churn, improving to 3% by month 18 as we improve onboarding"
- "Paid acquisition assumes $50 CAC at current channel efficiency"

### Expense Projection Model

```
Fixed Monthly Costs:
├── Cloud Infrastructure: $500 → scaling with customers
├── Software Tools: $300
├── Insurance: $200
├── Accounting: $500
└── Legal (amortized): $200

Variable Costs (per customer):
├── AI API: $2.50/customer/month
├── Hosting increment: $0.50/customer/month
├── Payment processing: 2.9% + $0.30 per transaction
└── Support: $1/customer/month (at scale)

Growth Investments (from funding):
├── Engineering hires: $8K-12K/month each
├── Marketing spend: ramp from $2K → $10K/month
└── Founder salary: $5K/month initially
```

### Three Scenarios

Always present three scenarios:
- **Conservative**: Lower conversion, higher churn, slower growth
- **Base**: Your best estimate with stated assumptions
- **Optimistic**: Higher conversion, viral growth, expansion revenue

Investors will mentally discount your base case by 30-50%. Presenting three scenarios shows you've thought about range of outcomes.

## Cap Table Basics

### What Is a Cap Table?

A capitalization table tracks who owns what percentage of your company. Before raising, you need a clean, well-structured cap table.

### Pre-Investment Cap Table (Solo Founder)

```
Shareholder          Shares     Percentage
─────────────────────────────────────────
Founder              8,000,000   80%
Option Pool          2,000,000   20%
─────────────────────────────────────────
Total               10,000,000  100%
```

The option pool is reserved for future employees and advisors. Investors typically require a 10-20% option pool BEFORE their investment (so it dilutes you, not them).

### Post-Investment Cap Table

```
Pre-Seed: $500K on $2M pre-money valuation
Post-money valuation: $2.5M
Investor ownership: $500K / $2.5M = 20%

Shareholder          Shares      Percentage
──────────────────────────────────────────
Founder              8,000,000    64%
Option Pool          2,000,000    16%
Pre-Seed Investors   2,500,000    20%
──────────────────────────────────────────
Total               12,500,000   100%
```

### Key Cap Table Concepts

**Pre-money valuation**: What your company is worth BEFORE the investment.

**Post-money valuation**: Pre-money + investment amount.

**Dilution**: Each funding round reduces existing shareholders' percentage (though the value per share should increase if the company is growing).

**Pro-rata rights**: Existing investors' right to maintain their ownership percentage in future rounds by investing proportionally.

**Anti-dilution protection**: Protects investors if you raise a future round at a lower valuation ("down round"). Weighted average is more founder-friendly than full ratchet.

### Cap Table Tools

- **Carta**: Industry standard. $$$. Worth it for Series A+.
- **Pulley**: Simpler, cheaper. Good for pre-seed/seed.
- **Captable.io**: Free basic tier. Good for getting started.
- **Spreadsheet**: Acceptable pre-fundraise but migrate to a real tool before closing.

## Term Sheet Understanding

### What Is a Term Sheet?

A term sheet is a non-binding document outlining the key terms of an investment. It's the "handshake agreement" before lawyers draft the actual contracts. Understanding every term is critical — this is where founder-unfriendly deals are born.

### Key Terms to Understand

**Valuation**
- Pre-money valuation determines how much of your company the investor gets
- Higher pre-money = less dilution for you
- But an unrealistically high valuation sets expectations that are hard to meet in the next round

**Investment Amount**
- Total capital being invested
- May come from one investor (lead) or a syndicate

**Type of Security**
- **Preferred Stock**: Standard for priced rounds. Investors get preference over common stockholders.
- **SAFE (Simple Agreement for Future Equity)**: Common for pre-seed. Not a priced round — converts to equity later. Y Combinator standardized these.
- **Convertible Note**: Debt that converts to equity. Has an interest rate and maturity date.

**Liquidation Preference**
- Determines who gets paid first in a sale or liquidation
- "1x non-participating" is standard and founder-friendly: investors get their money back OR their pro-rata share, whichever is more
- "1x participating" (double dip): investors get their money back AND their pro-rata share — avoid this
- "2x+" liquidation preference: investors get 2x+ their money back before anyone else — RED FLAG

**Board Seats**
- Who controls the board of directors
- Pre-seed/seed: typically no board seat for investors, or 1 seat with founder controlling the board
- Series A: common for lead investor to get a board seat (2 founders, 1 investor, 2 independents is reasonable)
- Never give up board control before Series B

**Vesting**
- Investors may require founder shares to vest (usually 4 years with 1-year cliff)
- If you've been working on this for 2 years, negotiate for credit for time served
- Standard: 4-year vesting, 1-year cliff, monthly vesting after cliff

**Anti-dilution**
- Broad-based weighted average is standard and fair
- Full ratchet is aggressive — negotiate against it

**Drag-along Rights**
- If majority shareholders agree to sell, they can "drag along" minority shareholders
- Standard provision, but understand the threshold (usually 50% or 66%)

**Right of First Refusal (ROFR)**
- Company and/or investors have the right to buy shares before they're sold to outsiders
- Standard and reasonable for early-stage companies

**Information Rights**
- Investors' right to receive financial reports (monthly/quarterly)
- Standard: quarterly financial statements, annual audit (if required)
- Be prepared to deliver monthly updates regardless — it's good practice

## Due Diligence Preparation

### What Investors Will Request

When an investor is serious, they'll send a due diligence checklist. Being prepared accelerates the process and demonstrates competence.

### Corporate Documents
- [ ] Articles of Incorporation / Certificate of Formation
- [ ] Operating Agreement (LLC) or Bylaws (Corp)
- [ ] Good standing certificate from your state
- [ ] EIN confirmation letter
- [ ] Board resolutions and minutes
- [ ] Cap table (current and pro forma)
- [ ] Any prior investment documents (SAFEs, notes, term sheets)

### Financial Documents
- [ ] Historical financial statements (P&L, Balance Sheet, Cash Flow)
- [ ] Bank statements (last 12 months)
- [ ] Tax returns (last 2-3 years)
- [ ] Current MRR/ARR documentation
- [ ] Customer list with revenue by customer
- [ ] Stripe dashboard access or export
- [ ] Financial model with projections and assumptions
- [ ] Burn rate and runway calculation

### Legal Documents
- [ ] All contracts with customers (especially enterprise)
- [ ] Terms of Service and Privacy Policy
- [ ] Contractor agreements with IP assignment clauses
- [ ] Employee offer letters and agreements
- [ ] Any IP filings (patents, trademarks)
- [ ] Insurance policies
- [ ] Any pending or threatened litigation

### Product and Technical
- [ ] Product roadmap
- [ ] Technical architecture overview
- [ ] Key metrics dashboard
- [ ] User growth and engagement data
- [ ] Churn analysis
- [ ] Competitive analysis
- [ ] Customer testimonials or case studies

### Organizing Due Diligence Materials

Create a secure data room (Google Drive folder with restricted access, or a dedicated tool like DocSend, Notion, or Carta):

```
/Due Diligence Data Room
├── /01-Corporate
│   ├── Articles of Incorporation.pdf
│   ├── Operating Agreement.pdf

├── /02-Financial
│   ├── Financial Statements 2024-2026.xlsx
│   ├── Tax Returns/

├── /03-Legal
│   ├── Terms of Service.pdf
│   ├── Privacy Policy.pdf

│   └── Metrics Dashboard Export.pdf

    ├── Customer List (anonymized).xlsx
    ├── Testimonials.pdf
    └── Case Studies/
```

## Valuation Methods

### For Pre-Revenue / Early Revenue

**Comparable transactions**: What did similar companies raise at? Check Crunchbase, PitchBook, or AngelList for comparable deals in your space, stage, and geography.

**Scorecard method**: Rate your startup against "average" funded startups on:
- Team strength (0-30% weight)
- Market size (0-25% weight)
- Product/technology (0-15% weight)
- Competitive environment (0-10% weight)
- Marketing/sales channels (0-10% weight)
- Need for additional investment (0-5% weight)
- Other factors (0-5% weight)

Multiply the average pre-seed/seed valuation in your area by your total score.

**Berkus method**: Assign values (up to $500K each) for:
- Sound idea (basic value)
- Prototype (technology risk reduction)
- Quality team (execution risk reduction)
- Strategic relationships (market risk reduction)
- Product rollout or sales (production risk reduction)

Maximum pre-revenue valuation: $2.5M

### For Revenue-Generating Startups

**Revenue multiple**: Common for SaaS.
- Pre-seed/Seed: 10-20x ARR (for high-growth)
- Series A: 15-30x ARR
- These multiples vary wildly based on growth rate, market, and market conditions

**Growth-adjusted multiple**: Multiply your ARR growth rate by your revenue multiple. A company growing 3x year-over-year typically commands higher multiples than one growing 1.5x.

**DCF (Discounted Cash Flow)**: Rarely used for early-stage. The projections are too uncertain. But understanding it shows financial sophistication.

### Valuation Negotiation

- Your valuation is ultimately what the market (investors) will pay
- Higher isn't always better — a too-high valuation creates pressure to perform
- Focus on finding the right investor, not the highest valuation
- Consider: would you rather have 80% of a $2M company or 60% of a $5M company? (Answer: depends on which one will be worth $100M)

## The Fundraising Process

### Timeline

| Phase | Duration | Activities |
|---|---|---|
| Preparation | 2-3 months | Materials, data room, target list |
| Warm intros | 2-4 weeks | Get introductions to target investors |
| First meetings | 4-6 weeks | Initial pitch meetings |
| Follow-up | 2-4 weeks | Deep dives, partner meetings |
| Term sheet | 1-2 weeks | Negotiate terms |
| Due diligence | 2-4 weeks | Investor verifies everything |
| Legal / Close | 2-4 weeks | Lawyers draft and finalize documents |
| **Total** | **4-6 months** | From prep to money in bank |

### Target List

Build a list of 50-100 potential investors:
- Angels who invest in your space
- Micro-VCs (pre-seed/seed focused)
- Seed-stage VCs
- Strategic angels (operators in your industry)

Research each one:
- What stage do they invest at?
- What sectors do they focus on?
- What's their typical check size?
- Have they invested in competitors?
- Who in your network can introduce you?

### The Warm Introduction

Cold emails to investors have a <1% response rate. Warm introductions have a 30-50% meeting rate. Every investor on your list should be reached through:
- Portfolio company founders who can vouch for you
- Other founders who have pitched them
- Mutual connections on LinkedIn
- Accelerator networks (YC, Techstars alumni)
- Angel networks

### Monthly Investor Updates

Start sending monthly updates 3-6 months BEFORE you plan to raise. This builds relationship and lets investors watch your progress.

**Update template:**
```
Subject: [Company] Monthly Update — March 2026

Hi [Name],

Highlights:
• MRR: $XX (up XX% from last month)
• New feature: [what you shipped]
• Key win: [notable customer, partnership, or milestone]

Challenges:
• [Honest assessment of what's hard]

Ask:
• [Specific ask — intro to someone, advice on something]

Thanks,
[Your name]
```

Keep it short. Send it consistently. Investors remember founders who are transparent and consistent.

## Common Fundraising Mistakes

### 1. Raising Too Early
If you don't have traction, you'll give up too much equity for too little money. Bootstrap until you have proof of concept and initial traction.

### 2. Raising Too Little
Underfunding means you'll be back fundraising in 6 months instead of building. Raise enough for 18-24 months of runway.

### 3. Raising Too Much
Overfunding at a low stage creates pressure and sets expectations. Raise what you need plus a buffer, not 10x what you need.

### 4. Not Knowing Your Numbers
If an investor asks your CAC, LTV, churn rate, or burn rate and you don't know instantly, the meeting is effectively over.

### 5. Ignoring Unit Economics
Growing revenue while losing money on each customer is not a viable business. Know your unit economics cold.

### 6. Single-Threaded Fundraising
Don't talk to one investor at a time. Run a parallel process — multiple conversations create urgency and competition.

### 7. Not Having a Clean Cap Table
SAFEs with confusing terms, missing IP assignments from contractors, informal "handshake" equity agreements — all of these kill deals. Clean it up before you start.

### 8. Overselling and Under-Delivering
Investors talk to each other. If your numbers don't hold up under due diligence, word spreads. Be honest and precise.

## Key Takeaways

1. **Start preparing 6+ months before you raise** — materials, data room, relationships
2. **Your pitch deck tells a story** — problem, solution, traction, team, ask
3. **Traction is the most important slide** — numbers beat narratives
4. **Financial projections must be bottom-up** — with clearly stated assumptions
5. **Understand every term in a term sheet** — liquidation preference and board control matter most
6. **Keep a clean cap table** — use real tools, not napkin math
7. **Build your data room before you need it** — due diligence readiness signals competence
8. **Warm introductions are mandatory** — cold outreach doesn't work for fundraising
9. **Know your numbers cold** — CAC, LTV, churn, burn rate, runway
10. **Monthly investor updates build relationships** — start before you raise
